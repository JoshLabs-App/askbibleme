## Preface

Scripture is truly without equal. Early twentieth-century evangelist Billy Sunday pictured the Bible as a magnificent palace—from the porch of Genesis, through the gallery of the Old Testament, the music room of Psalms, the watchtower of the prophets, to the throne room of the King of kings. When everything else is false, Christ alone is true.

The Bible inspires reverence. Yet we must not only admire it—we must **understand** it. Most people who have strong opinions about the Bible, whether positive or negative, do not understand what it actually says. Knowing Scripture matters because it is God's word; that becomes clearer the more you study it.

We therefore need to know **how** to study the Bible. That involves four things: **reading Scripture, interpreting Scripture, meditating on Scripture, and teaching Scripture**.

---

## 1. Reading Scripture

Bible study begins with reading the Bible itself. Frankly, most people never truly do this. At best they nibble at the text. They may read books *about* the Bible or devotional material loosely based on Scripture—but they are not reading Scripture itself.

Good Christian books and magazines can supplement your study, but **nothing replaces reading the Bible itself**.

### The Old Testament

Christians should aim to read through the entire Old Testament once a year. There are thirty-nine books; if you read about twenty minutes a day, you can finish in a year.

The Old Testament was written in Hebrew—a relatively straightforward language, unlike the nuanced Greek of the New Testament. It is not primarily abstract or philosophical; it is concrete and vivid.

The Old Testament is largely historical narrative, interwoven with law, poetry, and prophecy. Read straight through from Genesis to Malachi. Mark passages you do not understand in the margin with a pencil. Over time you will erase many of those marks—as you read again and again, your understanding grows and earlier questions resolve. (For what remains unclear, consult commentaries or other helps later.)

One reason the Old Testament can feel confusing is that it is not always in strict chronological order. A chronologically arranged Bible (such as *The Narrated Bible*) can help—you will see, for example, David's psalms placed alongside events in his life, and the prophets linked to the kings of their day.

**The point: read the Old Testament straight through, regularly.** You will be surprised what you learn, as the New Testament says: "For whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope." (Romans 15:4)

### The New Testament

The New Testament calls for a different approach. In Colossians 1:25–26 Paul speaks of the mystery now revealed to the saints; in Ephesians 3:3–5 he describes himself as apostle of that mystery. The New Testament fulfills and interprets the Old Testament and brings us to God's fullest revelation. **You should spend more time in the New Testament**, because it explains the Old. Moreover, the New Testament was written in Greek—a richly nuanced language of abstract ideas and subtle meaning—so it demands extra diligence.

### A method: reading New Testament books repeatedly

In seminary MacArthur decided to read through 1 John every day for thirty days. On day one, read all five chapters (about twenty to thirty minutes); repeat on days two and three. By day seven or eight you may think, "I'm not getting anything new—I understand 1 John now." If you keep going through the month, your grasp of 1 John will deepen remarkably.

He uses the same method when preparing messages: read the passage again and again until it fills his mind.

**Suggestion:** As you read 1 John, write each chapter's theme on a 3×5 card. Each day, review the card and recite the themes. Soon you will know the outline by heart.

After 1 John, move to a longer book. **John's Gospel** is a natural next step because you already know John's style. Divide its twenty-one chapters into three blocks of seven chapters, thirty days each. After ninety days you will have a solid hold on John—while continuing the Old Testament and keeping themes on cards.

Then you might return to a shorter book (e.g. Philippians), read Matthew, Colossians, Acts, and so on—alternating longer and shorter books, thirty days each. In about **two and a half years you can read the entire New Testament thirty times**.

Read the New Testament in a way that helps you **remember** it. You will not forget what you read days ago; you will not need a concordance to find familiar passages. If you keep renewing your mind with Scripture, the Bible will stay with you for life.

With this repetitive method, use the **same Bible and the same translation** so you internalize exact wording and location. Occasionally reading another translation can also bring fresh light.

MacArthur usually reads the King James Version; for study he also reads the New American Standard Bible (very literal) and the English Standard Version (clear and readable).

Repeated reading dramatically increases your overall knowledge of Scripture—because **Scripture interprets Scripture**. God did not give us the Bible to trip us up, but to be understood.

Some say, "Read anything but Revelation—it's too confusing." Yet Revelation 1:3 says, "Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it." God wants us to read it—but you will not fully grasp Revelation until you have read Daniel, Isaiah, and Ezekiel. When you read all of God's word, the pieces begin to fit together.

## Deep Reading · Companion plan

On AskBible.me we built **[NT deep repeat · OT read-through](/read/plans/nt-deep-repeat)** from the most demanding—and most rewarding—part of MacArthur’s method (full overview: [AskBible reading approach](/explore/articles/article_1778108127353_fzymbc)):

- **Old Testament:** One chapter per day from Genesis through Malachi in canonical order; when you finish, loop back—no catch-up, no guilt.
- **New Testament:** MacArthur’s book order, shaped into **52 stages** (usually 4–6 chapters each). “N passes” = **N days per stage** (7 / 14 / 28)—one round through all 52 stages ≈ **12 months / 2 years / 4 years**.
- **Daily rhythm:** New Testament stage first, then one Old Testament chapter; the two tracks advance independently.

This is not a 365-day calendar or a make-up schedule—**the day you set this plan active is day 1**, starting at stage 1 (1 John) and Genesis 1.

## 2. Interpreting Scripture

Once you have read Scripture and know what it says, seek to understand what it **means**. Only when you can interpret a passage correctly can you apply it to your life and bring glory to God.

Nehemiah 8 shows the pattern: Ezra brought the Law before the assembly and read from early morning until midday. The people listened attentively; when Ezra opened the book, they stood and worshiped the Lord.

**Verse 8 is key:** "They read from the book, from the Law of God, clearly, and they gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading." That is what explaining Scripture means.

In 1 Timothy 4:13 Paul says, "Devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture, to exhortation, to teaching." That is what it means to "rightly handle the word of truth" (2 Timothy 2:15). Any deviation leads to distortion—and distortion is the source of all kinds of fanaticism.

### What to avoid

#### Do not twist Scripture to prove your point

Do not put your own meaning on the text and call it God's. Do not hunt for verses to support ideas you already hold. In 2 Corinthians 2:17 Paul says, "We are not, like so many, peddlers of God's word." The Greek *kapeleuo* means selling in the market by deceit—promising one thing and delivering another. Do not corrupt Scripture to preach your predetermined sermon.

#### Avoid shallow Bible study

Productive study requires someone to have worked on the text beforehand so the group can discuss and apply it wisely. Interpretation demands diligent investigation; do not take shortcuts by believing whatever anyone says the Bible means. Examine the facts yourself. Do not assume a verse has many equally valid interpretations. Application may be multi-faceted; **meaning** is one. God's word is precise, not vague.

#### Do not allegorize the text

Never treat the Bible as a code of hidden meanings unrelated to what the text plainly says. Old Testament narrative is not a fairy tale to be spiritualized away. Scripture deserves rigorous respect.

### Bridging the gaps

Correct interpretation requires crossing several gaps:

#### Language

We speak Chinese or English; the Bible was written in Hebrew, Greek, and Aramaic. Modern translations are excellent, yet none conveys everything in the original.

In 1 Corinthians 4:1 Paul calls himself a "minister of Christ" and steward of God's mysteries. English *minister* suggests dignity—a prime minister or cabinet officer. The Greek *huperetes* originally meant a third-tier rower in a ship's hold—no prestige at all. Paul uses it to say he is Christ's lowly servant. English *minister* cannot carry that—you must cross the language gap.

Tools such as Vine's *Expository Dictionary of New Testament Words* and Strong's Concordance help trace word usage through Scripture.

#### Culture

Some books are four thousand years old. Without the culture of the original audience, you cannot grasp the meaning.

John 1:1 says, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." Why not simply "In the beginning was Jesus"? In that culture *logos* meant something profound to both Greeks (reason, cosmic order) and Jews (God's self-expression). By presenting Jesus as the personal Creator, John speaks to both audiences.

Without knowing Pharisees, Sadducees, and Jewish customs, Matthew is opaque. Without some grasp of Gnosticism, Colossians is hard to follow.

#### Geography

Scripture is full of geography—"down" to Jericho, "up" to Jerusalem. In 1 Thessalonians 1:8 Paul's gospel spread from Thessalonica because the city sat on the Via Egnatia, the main east–west route; news traveled along that road. A good atlas clarifies many passages.

#### History

Historical background enriches understanding. In John, Pilate's interaction with Jesus makes sense only if you know he had already provoked the Jews by forcing emperor worship and feared another riot that would ruin his career—hence he handed Jesus over to be crucified.

Bible dictionaries and encyclopedias supply reliable historical context.

### Steps to take

#### 1. Take the literal sense

Seek the normal, natural, plain meaning. Symbol and metaphor appear in Scripture, but context shows when they do. Prophetic books use symbolic images that convey real truth. Interpret as you would other literature—not by arbitrary letter-number games.

#### 2. Know the context

Study the historical setting: to whom was this written? Study the literary context: how does this passage relate to what surrounds it? **A text out of context is a pretext.**

#### 3. Analyze sentence structure

Diagram the sentence—verbs, nouns, participles—to see what the author emphasizes.

In the Great Commission (Matthew 28:19–20), "go," "baptize," and "teach" are participles modifying the one imperative: **"make disciples."** The commission is disciple-making that includes going, baptizing, and teaching.

#### 4. Compare with the rest of Scripture

The Reformation principle of *analogia Scriptura*: Scripture does not contradict itself. When 1 Corinthians 15:29 mentions baptism for the dead, that cannot mean a living person is baptized to save someone already dead—that would contradict salvation by grace through personal faith in Christ.

#### 5. Draw out principles for application

Reread until you see spiritual principles for yourself and other believers—built on literal meaning, context, structure, and consistency with all of Scripture.

## 3. Meditating on Scripture

Do not rush God's word. Deuteronomy 6:6–7: "These words shall be on your heart… when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise." **God's word should occupy your mind at all times.**

Meditation weaves individual teachings into a compact, unified grasp of truth. Biblical meditation is thoughtful, wise reflection—like a cow chewing the cud.

Psalm 1:1–2: "His delight is in the law of the Lord, and on his law he meditates day and night." Blessed is the one who meditates on Scripture continually.

---

## 4. Teaching Scripture

The best way to retain something is to give it away. To teach effectively you must master the subject—and teaching forces you to do so. Find someone eager to learn but less advanced, and systematically pass on what you know.

As you feed others, you feed yourself. A major motive for study is responsibility to teach. Without someone to teach, study may lag.

---

## 5. Final reminder

Having learned practical steps for reading, interpreting, meditating, and teaching Scripture, make each one a **lifelong habit**.

When you think you know it all, remember Deuteronomy 29:29: "The secret things belong to the Lord our God." We only touch the surface of God's infinite wisdom—yet the pursuit is worthwhile, because he has given us his word that we might know him.

We study not for knowledge that puffs up, as Paul says: "Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up." (1 Corinthians 8:1) We study to know God—and knowing God requires humility.
